The Six Assessment Dimensions

Every NSAG assessment has six dimensions — specific, observable, documentable governance criteria. For NARCH, those six dimensions are:

D1

Evidence Documentation Standards

Does the organization maintain citable, primary-source evidence documentation for specific design decisions? Can a design rationale be handed to a client, grant reviewer, or regulatory body with citations that can be verified?

Why it matters: Most architecture firms know what the research says in general. Almost none have a document that links a specific design intervention to a specific study with a specific evidence grade. That document is what makes the decision auditable.

D2

Biophilic Design Integration

What is the density and distribution of biophilic elements across three categories: direct (living plants, water features, natural light access), indirect (natural materials, organic patterns, nature imagery), and spatial (refuge spaces, prospect views, transition areas)?

Why it matters: Biophilic design is not one thing. Direct, indirect, and spatial elements have distinct evidence bases and distinct nervous system effects. An assessment that treats them as a single category misses the governance-relevant distinctions.

D3

Acoustic and Sensory Environment Governance

Are acoustic stressors — noise, reverberation, speech intelligibility — assessed and governed through documented design criteria? Are sensory inputs (light spectrum, thermal range, air quality) treated as design variables with evidence-based targets?

Why it matters: Acoustic exposure is among the most well-evidenced built environment stressors. Hospital noise at night causes measurable sleep disruption and cardiovascular stress. Workplaces with uncontrolled background noise show measurable working memory impairment. These are governance variables, not aesthetic preferences.

D4

Post-Occupancy Evaluation Framework

Does the organization have a structured framework for measuring whether a completed space is producing its intended cognitive, physiological, and behavioral effects? Are baseline and post-occupancy assessments built into the engagement model?

Why it matters: A biophilic design claim without a post-occupancy evaluation is an unverified hypothesis. Governance requires measurement. Most evidence-based design work ends at certificate of occupancy. NARCH assessments close this loop.

D5

Equity and Access Assessment

Who has access to the biophilic and sensory benefits designed into the space? Are the evidence-supported design elements concentrated in executive and public-facing areas while production, service, and administrative spaces receive standard treatment?

Why it matters: Biophilic equity is a documented governance problem. The research on nature access and health outcomes has a clear equity dimension: access to green space, natural light, and acoustic quality correlates with socioeconomic status in ways the built environment can either reinforce or interrupt.

D6

Grant and Approval Documentation Standards

Can the organization produce evidence documentation in formats usable for grant applications, public approval processes, and regulatory filings? Are evidence briefs structured for audiences who are not design professionals?

Why it matters: The audience for evidence documentation is not always a client. Grants, planning approvals, and healthcare accreditation processes require evidence at a different register. NARCH assessments produce documentation that works across all three contexts.

NSAG Principles in This Domain

The Biophilic Evidence Audit is not a standalone product — it deploys specific NSAG principles that shape every dimension of the assessment.

P5

The built environment is governance.

This is NARCH's foundational claim, stated as a universal principle. Physical space, acoustics, lighting, and nature access determine what is cognitively and physiologically possible. NARCH makes this principle actionable — auditable, documented, and defensible.

P8

Cognitive load is a design variable the system controls.

Wayfinding complexity, decision density, spatial ambiguity — these are design variables that produce measurable cognitive load effects. When patients fail to navigate healthcare environments, the environment has often created the conditions for that failure.

P15

What institutions cannot see, they cannot govern.

The visibility gap in built environment governance is the gap between what a design is intended to produce and what it is actually producing. Post-occupancy evaluation is the measurement infrastructure that closes this gap.

P16

Evidence without governance is inert.

Six hundred studies exist on the nervous system effects of the built environment. Most architecture firms cannot access them, interpret them, or translate them into client-facing documentation. NARCH is the governance layer that makes that evidence usable.

Signal vs. Noise

What Is Worth Paying Attention To

Signal

Biophilic design outcome studies in hospital settings (2018-2024)

The most rigorous evidence for nervous-system effects of built environments comes from healthcare — window views, natural light, acoustic design, and wayfinding clarity all have measurable outcome data applicable to non-healthcare contexts with appropriate translation. Primary sources: HERD, Health Environments Research and Design Journal.

Signal

GSA post-occupancy evaluation standards for federal buildings

The U.S. General Services Administration has developed post-occupancy evaluation frameworks that operationalize the connection between space and cognitive/physiological outcomes in ways that translate directly to evidence documentation for design rationale. Primary source: GSA PBS-P100 Facilities Standards.

Noise

Biophilic design trend reports from furniture and materials vendors

These documents cite real research but selection-bias the evidence toward findings that favor their product categories. The underlying studies are often credible; the curation is not neutral. Read the primary sources directly.

Noise

Wellness certifications as evidence of outcome

LEED, WELL, and similar certifications measure inputs and features, not nervous system outcomes. Certification is not evidence that the intended biological effects occurred. It is evidence that specified features were installed.

Terminology

NARCH Glossary

Language used precisely in this domain. These distinctions carry real analytical weight.

Cognitive affordance

A design feature that actively shapes what cognitive operations are easy, difficult, or impossible in a space. Distinct from aesthetic preference — a cognitive affordance has a measurable effect on attention, working memory, or stress regulation.

Restorative environment

A space that reliably reduces attentional fatigue and stress load over time. Defined in Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan and Kaplan, 1989) with reference to four properties: being away, extent, fascination, and compatibility. Not synonymous with relaxing.

Thermal comfort vs. thermal preference

Thermal comfort is an objective measure of the range within which the body maintains homeostasis without active thermoregulation. Thermal preference is subjective. Both matter to design; they are not the same measure and are often conflated in post-occupancy surveys.

Acoustic masking

A deliberate introduction of background sound to reduce the intelligibility of distracting speech — distinct from noise reduction and soundproofing. Each has different nervous system effects and different evidence bases.

Biophilic density

The ratio of nature-referencing elements (direct: living plants, water; indirect: natural patterns, materials; spatial: refuge, prospect) to total square footage. An operational measure for comparing design intensity to outcome expectations in the research literature.
